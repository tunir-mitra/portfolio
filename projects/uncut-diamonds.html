Uncut Diamonds

What building a platform during lockdown taught me about people, potential, and the gap between launching and sustaining.

In April 2020, millions of Indians were home, restless, and quietly terrified about what came next. I was one of them. But somewhere between the anxiety and the stillness, a question kept nagging at me: if people have time they've never had before, why aren't they using it to become who they've always wanted to be?

That question became Uncut Diamonds.

The Idea Behind the Name

A rough diamond looks like nothing. Grey, unimpressive, easy to overlook. But the potential locked inside it is extraordinary. It just needs the right conditions, the right pressure, the right hands.

That's exactly how I saw the people around me during the pandemic. Not as victims of circumstance, but as uncut talent waiting for a platform that took them seriously.

So my team of three set out to build one. Aryaman Chandra, Aranyak Nayak, and I had no playbook, no funding, and no guarantee the moment would last long enough to matter.

The Team

We Refused to Build "Just Another Course Platform"

The MOOC graveyard is full of well-intentioned platforms that forgot one thing: learning without opportunity is just expensive procrastination.

Uncut Diamonds was designed from day one as three interlocking things.

01 · Learn it

A Blended Learning Environment

Not just tech courses like Web Development and Data Skills, but courses that reflected the full range of human ambition: Cooking, Mental Health, Creative Writing. We believed skills were skills, and we weren't going to gatekeep which ones mattered.

→
02 · Prove it

A Talent Portal

Where recruiters could find vetted, actively-learning candidates. Not resumes. People mid-transformation.

→
03 · Use it

A Freelance Marketplace

So that the moment someone finished a course, they had somewhere to go with it. A place to advertise, pitch, and earn.

The ecosystem logic was simple: learn it, prove it, use it. All in one place.

Building at Lockdown Speed

Aryaman led the technical architecture. Aranyak owned partnerships and outreach. I led product and go-to-market: what we built, for whom, and how we reached them. None of those lines were ever clean, but the division gave us enough clarity to move fast.

We launched on Wix, a deliberate choice. Speed mattered more than infrastructure elegance in week one. We needed to be in people's hands before the moment passed.

Within 45 days, we had over 330 users. That number felt small and enormous at the same time.

Growth didn't come from ad spend. It came from meeting people where they were spending their locked-down hours: Instagram, Facebook, and oddly enough, through partnerships with food delivery services whose riders had time between orders and zero structured upskilling options. That was our most unexpected insight. The gig economy wasn't just a user base; it was an underserved classroom.

The gig economy wasn't just a user base. It was an underserved classroom.

We ran A/B tests obsessively on onboarding flows and navigation. A feedback loop built from Google Forms and on-site pop-ups, unglamorous tools used relentlessly, pushed our signup-to-engagement conversion up 19%. We iterated in real time because we had no choice, and because our users were generous enough to tell us what wasn't working.

What the Numbers Didn't Show

Here's what I won't pretend: the launch was the easy part.

User acquisition has a certain intoxicating momentum to it. You watch the numbers climb and mistake motion for progress. What we discovered, painfully and slowly, was that sustaining a service ecosystem is a completely different problem from launching one.

These weren't afterthoughts. They were the whole game.
Information architectureInstructor quality controlLong-term learner retention

And we'd spent most of our energy on the opening move.

The platform worked. The business model needed real work.

That gap, between a product that catches fire and a system that sustains it, is where I learned the most. Not from the wins, but from the moment the wins plateaued and we had to ask harder questions.

What Rough Diamonds Actually Teach You

The irony of building Uncut Diamonds is that I came out the other side feeling like one myself. Full of potential, but aware of exactly how much cutting was still needed.

The pandemic didn't create the skills gap in India. It just made it impossible to look away from. The people who showed up on our platform weren't looking for a certificate. They were looking for a door.

  • Mid-career professionals
  • Delivery riders
  • Recent graduates
  • Homemakers

We opened a few. I want to open more.

The Part Nobody Talks About

As the world came out of COVID, priorities shifted. The VIT incubation center that had backed us began directing its energy toward newer projects. Funding slowed, then stopped. And without that foundation, Uncut Diamonds couldn't sustain itself.

We shut it down. Handed everything over. Walked away from something we'd poured a year of our lives into.

There's no elegant way to describe that feeling. It wasn't failure in the dramatic sense. The platform worked, the users were real, the problem we were solving was real. It was more like watching a good idea outgrow the conditions that made it possible, and not yet having the resources to build new ones.

Startups born in crisis often don't survive the return to normalcy. That's a pattern, not a personal failing. But it takes a while to see it that way.

We opened a few doors. I want to open more, and this time, build the walls too.

The question hasn't gone away. What does a platform look like that doesn't just teach skills, but actually changes trajectories? And what does it take to keep it standing long after the crisis that inspired it has passed?

I'm still working on both answers.
